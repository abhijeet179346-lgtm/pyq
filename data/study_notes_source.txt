@@Physics|Motion In One Dimension|Kinematics in a straight line
CORE IDEAS
Displacement is the change in position; distance is the length of the path. Velocity is the rate of displacement and acceleration is the rate of velocity. The slope of a position-time graph gives velocity; signed area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement.
FORMULAS
For constant acceleration: v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A particle starts at 4 m/s and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 3 s. Its final speed is 10 m/s and displacement is 4×3 + ½×2×9 = 21 m.
COMMON MISTAKE
These equations require constant acceleration. Use integration when acceleration depends on time or position.
@@Physics|Motion In Two Dimensions|Vectors and projectiles
CORE IDEAS
Resolve vectors into independent perpendicular components. For an ideal projectile, horizontal acceleration is zero and vertical acceleration is downward g. The horizontal velocity remains constant.
FORMULAS
x = u cosθ t; y = u sinθ t − ½gt². For landing at launch height: T = 2u sinθ/g; R = u² sin2θ/g; H = u² sin²θ/(2g).
WORKED EXAMPLE
With u = 20 m/s, θ = 30° and g = 10 m/s², flight time is 2 s and range is 20√3 m.
COMMON MISTAKE
The standard range formula does not apply when launch and landing heights differ.
@@Physics|Laws of Motion|Forces and friction
CORE IDEAS
Draw a free-body diagram for each body. Choose axes before resolving forces. Newton's second law applies to the net external force in an inertial frame.
FORMULAS
ΣF = ma; static friction satisfies |f| ≤ μsN; limiting static friction = μsN; kinetic friction = μkN.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A 2 kg block on a horizontal table has μk = 0.2. With g = 10 m/s², kinetic friction is 4 N. A horizontal applied force of 10 N then gives acceleration (10−4)/2 = 3 m/s².
COMMON MISTAKE
Static friction is not always μsN; it adjusts until the limiting value is reached.
@@Physics|Work Power Energy|Energy methods
CORE IDEAS
The work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy. Mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces do work. Power describes the rate of doing work.
FORMULAS
Wnet = Δ(½mv²); gravitational potential near Earth = mgh; spring potential = ½kx²; instantaneous power = F·v.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A 1 kg body falls through 5 m from rest with negligible air resistance. For g = 10 m/s², it gains 50 J of kinetic energy and reaches 10 m/s.
COMMON MISTAKE
A force perpendicular to instantaneous displacement does no work, even if it changes the direction of motion.
@@Physics|Rotational Motion|Torque and angular momentum
CORE IDEAS
Torque measures the turning effect of a force. The moment of inertia depends on the axis. Angular momentum is conserved about a point when the net external torque about that point vanishes.
FORMULAS
τ = r×F; τ = Iα about a fixed axis; L = Iω for fixed-axis rotation; Krot = ½Iω²; parallel-axis theorem: I = Icm + Md².
WORKED EXAMPLE
A uniform disc of mass 2 kg and radius 0.5 m has I = ½MR² = 0.25 kg m². At 4 rad/s, rotational energy is 2 J.
COMMON MISTAKE
Use the same axis when calculating torque, angular momentum and moment of inertia.
@@Physics|Gravitation|Orbits and escape
CORE IDEAS
A circular satellite orbit uses gravity as the centripetal force. Gravitational potential energy is zero at infinite separation by convention and is negative for a bound pair.
FORMULAS
F = GMm/r²; U = −GMm/r; circular speed = √(GM/r); circular-orbit energy = −GMm/(2r); escape speed = √(2GM/r).
WORKED EXAMPLE
If the radius of a circular orbit becomes four times larger, speed becomes one-half and the orbital period becomes eight times larger.
COMMON MISTAKE
Use distance from Earth's centre, not height above the surface, in orbital formulas.
@@Physics|Electrostatics|Electric field and potential
CORE IDEAS
Electric field is a vector; potential is a scalar. Fields add vectorially while potentials add algebraically. A positive test charge accelerates in the electric field direction.
FORMULAS
For a point charge: E = kq/r² radially; V = kq/r; potential energy of two charges = kq₁q₂/r. In a uniform field, the potential decreases along the field.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Two equal positive point charges placed symmetrically about the origin give zero net field at the origin but a positive potential there.
COMMON MISTAKE
A point with zero electric field need not have zero potential.
@@Physics|Capacitance|Capacitor networks
CORE IDEAS
Capacitance relates stored charge to potential difference. Parallel capacitors share voltage; series capacitors carry equal charge magnitude when initially uncharged and connected in a simple series circuit.
FORMULAS
C = Q/V; parallel: Ceq = ΣC; series: 1/Ceq = Σ(1/C); U = ½CV² = Q²/(2C).
WORKED EXAMPLE
Capacitors of 2 μF and 3 μF in parallel give 5 μF. Across 10 V, they store a total charge of 50 μC and energy 250 μJ.
COMMON MISTAKE
Whether a battery remains connected changes how voltage, charge and energy respond to inserting a dielectric.
@@Physics|Current Electricity|Ohm law and circuit power
CORE IDEAS
Current is charge flow per unit time. Resistances in series share current; resistances in parallel share voltage. An ideal ammeter has negligible resistance and an ideal voltmeter has very high resistance.
FORMULAS
V = IR; R = ρL/A; P = VI = I²R = V²/R for an ohmic resistor.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A 6 Ω and a 3 Ω resistor in parallel have equivalent resistance 2 Ω. A 12 V source supplies 6 A and 72 W.
COMMON MISTAKE
The voltage across a real cell's terminals is not always equal to its EMF because of internal resistance.
@@Physics|Magnetic Effects of Current|Magnetic force
CORE IDEAS
A magnetic field exerts force on a moving charge perpendicular to its velocity and the field. It does no work on a point charge. A current-carrying conductor also experiences magnetic force.
FORMULAS
F = qv×B; for perpendicular motion, radius r = mv/(|q|B); force on a straight wire = Iℓ×B; long straight wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr).
WORKED EXAMPLE
If a charged particle's speed doubles in a fixed perpendicular field, its orbit radius doubles while its cyclotron angular frequency remains |q|B/m in the nonrelativistic limit.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reverse the direction given by the right-hand rule for a negative charge.
@@Physics|Thermodynamics|Heat work and ideal gases
CORE IDEAS
State the work convention before using the first law. With W defined as work done by the gas, ΔU = Q−W. Internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature.
FORMULAS
PV = nRT; isothermal ideal-gas expansion: ΔU = 0; reversible isothermal work W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁). Reversible adiabatic: PV^γ = constant.
WORKED EXAMPLE
If a gas absorbs 100 J of heat and does 40 J of work, its internal energy rises by 60 J.
COMMON MISTAKE
An isothermal process is not necessarily adiabatic. In an isothermal expansion an ideal gas may absorb heat while doing work.
@@Physics|Ray Optics|Lenses and image formation
CORE IDEAS
Choose and consistently follow a sign convention. For a thin lens using the Cartesian convention, object distance to the left is negative and a real image on the right has positive image distance.
FORMULAS
1/f = 1/v − 1/u; magnification m = v/u; power P = 1/f when f is in metres. Powers of thin lenses in contact add.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For a convex lens with f = 10 cm and u = −30 cm, 1/v = 1/10−1/30 = 1/15, so v = 15 cm and m = −0.5.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not mix the mirror equation and the lens equation or switch sign conventions part-way through.
@@Physics|Wave Optics|Interference and polarisation
CORE IDEAS
Stable interference requires coherent sources. Bright fringes correspond to integer wavelength path differences; dark fringes correspond to odd half-wavelength differences for equal coherent sources.
FORMULAS
Young double-slit fringe spacing β = λD/d. Malus law: I = I₀cos²θ for initially plane-polarised light. An ideal polariser transmits half the intensity of unpolarised light.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Polarised light of intensity I₀ passes through an analyser at 60°. The transmitted intensity is I₀/4.
COMMON MISTAKE
Apply the initial half-intensity factor only when unpolarised light enters the first ideal polariser.
@@Physics|Atomic Physics|Photoelectric effect and energy levels
CORE IDEAS
A photon transfers energy hν. Photoemission occurs only above a threshold frequency. Higher intensity increases photon count; it does not increase the maximum photoelectron energy at fixed frequency.
FORMULAS
Kmax = hν−φ = eVs; hydrogen-like energy levels En = −13.6Z²/n² eV; de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A 5 eV photon incident on a surface of work function 2 eV gives maximum kinetic energy 3 eV and stopping potential 3 V.
COMMON MISTAKE
Convert eV to joules when combining energies with SI values of h or particle mass.
@@Chemistry|Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry|Moles and limiting reagents
CORE IDEAS
Balance the equation before comparing reactant amounts. Convert each available reactant amount into the maximum product amount it can produce. The smallest value identifies the limiting reagent.
FORMULAS
n = m/M; number of particles = nNA; molarity = moles of solute/litres of solution.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, 3 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂ can produce 3 mol and 2 mol water respectively. Oxygen limits the reaction and 2 mol water forms, leaving 1 mol H₂.
COMMON MISTAKE
Compare stoichiometric mole ratios, not the masses of reactants directly.
@@Chemistry|Structure of Atom|Quantum numbers and electronic structure
CORE IDEAS
An orbital is specified by n, l and ml. An electron also has spin quantum number ms. Pauli's principle allows at most two electrons per orbital with opposite spins.
KEY RELATIONS
For a shell n, l ranges from 0 to n−1. For a subshell l, ml ranges from −l to +l. A subshell contains 2l+1 orbitals and accommodates 2(2l+1) electrons.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A d subshell has l = 2, five orbitals and a maximum capacity of ten electrons.
COMMON MISTAKE
An orbital is not a classical circular trajectory. Distinguish orbital count from electron capacity.
@@Chemistry|Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure|Bonding geometry and molecular orbitals
CORE IDEAS
VSEPR uses electron domains around a central atom to predict geometry. Lone pairs affect molecular shape. Molecular orbital theory distinguishes bonding and antibonding electrons.
FORMULA
Bond order = (number of bonding electrons − number of antibonding electrons)/2. Unpaired electrons imply paramagnetism in the usual introductory molecular-orbital treatment.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Water has four electron domains around oxygen: two bonds and two lone pairs. The electron-domain arrangement is approximately tetrahedral, but the molecular shape is bent.
COMMON MISTAKE
Electron-domain geometry and molecular shape are different descriptions when lone pairs are present.
@@Chemistry|Chemical Equilibrium|Equilibrium constants
CORE IDEAS
At equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, but concentrations need not be equal. The reaction quotient Q is evaluated using the current composition and compared with K.
FORMULA
For aA+bB ⇌ cC+dD, Kc = [C]^c[D]^d/([A]^a[B]^b). Pure solids and liquids have unit activity and are omitted from the usual equilibrium expression.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For H₂+I₂ ⇌ 2HI, concentrations 0.2, 0.2 and 0.8 mol/L at equilibrium give Kc = 0.8²/(0.2×0.2) = 16.
COMMON MISTAKE
A catalyst changes how quickly equilibrium is reached, not the equilibrium constant at a given temperature.
@@Chemistry|Ionic Equilibrium|Acids bases and buffers
CORE IDEAS
Acid-base calculations depend on dissociation strength and concentration. A buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.
FORMULAS
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; at 25°C, Kw ≈ 10⁻¹⁴. For an appropriate weak-acid buffer, pH ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]).
WORKED EXAMPLE
A buffer with equal concentrations of a weak acid and its conjugate base has pH approximately equal to pKa.
COMMON MISTAKE
The Henderson-Hasselbalch approximation is inappropriate after the buffer capacity has been exceeded or when dissociation corrections dominate.
@@Chemistry|Thermodynamics (C)|Enthalpy entropy and Gibbs energy
CORE IDEAS
Enthalpy change describes heat transfer at constant pressure when only pressure-volume work is involved. Entropy measures the dispersal of energy and accessible microscopic states. Spontaneity depends on the system and surroundings.
FORMULA
At constant temperature and pressure: ΔG = ΔH−TΔS. A negative ΔG favours the forward process under the stated conditions.
WORKED EXAMPLE
If ΔH = 20 kJ/mol and ΔS = 100 J/(mol K), then at 300 K, ΔG = 20−30 = −10 kJ/mol.
COMMON MISTAKE
Convert entropy to compatible energy units before calculating TΔS. A spontaneous process need not be fast.
@@Chemistry|Chemical Kinetics|Rate laws and half-life
CORE IDEAS
The rate law is determined experimentally and does not generally follow directly from the balanced overall equation. Reaction order is the sum of concentration exponents in the rate law.
FORMULAS
First order: ln([A]₀/[A]t) = kt; t½ = ln2/k. Arrhenius equation: k = Aexp(−Ea/RT).
WORKED EXAMPLE
A first-order reaction with k = 0.0693 min⁻¹ has half-life 10 min. After 20 min, one-quarter of the initial reactant remains.
COMMON MISTAKE
The concentration-independent half-life formula applies to first-order kinetics, not every reaction.
@@Chemistry|Electrochemistry|Cells and electrolysis
CORE IDEAS
Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode in both galvanic and electrolytic cells. Electrode signs depend on the cell type.
FORMULAS
Ecell = Ecathode−Eanode when both are tabulated as reduction potentials. ΔG° = −nFE°. Electrolysis: deposited amount n(metal) = Q/(zF).
WORKED EXAMPLE
Passing one Faraday through a solution containing Cu²⁺ deposits one-half mole of copper when deposition is the only cathodic process.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not multiply a standard electrode potential when multiplying a half-reaction; potential is an intensive quantity.
@@Chemistry|Coordination Compounds|Ligands oxidation states and magnetism
CORE IDEAS
A ligand donates an electron pair to a central metal. Coordination number counts donor atoms directly bonded to the metal. Oxidation state follows from ligand charges and total complex charge.
FORMULA
For spin-only estimates, magnetic moment μ ≈ √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons.
WORKED EXAMPLE
In [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺, ammonia is neutral, so cobalt is +3. The coordination number is six. Determining the spin state also requires the d-electron count and ligand-field information.
COMMON MISTAKE
The number of ligands need not equal the coordination number for multidentate ligands.
@@Chemistry|General Organic Chemistry|Electronic effects and reaction reasoning
CORE IDEAS
Inductive effects act through sigma bonds; resonance involves delocalisation of electrons across suitable conjugated orbitals. Identify nucleophiles, electrophiles and the bonds changing in a reaction.
METHOD
First compare possible intermediates, then examine resonance, inductive effects, hyperconjugation, solvent and steric constraints. Use curved arrows from an electron pair or bond toward an electron-deficient site.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A resonance-stabilised carboxylate conjugate base helps explain why carboxylic acids are generally more acidic than comparable alcohols.
COMMON MISTAKE
Resonance structures are alternative drawings of one delocalised species, not separate molecules interconverting in equilibrium.
@@Chemistry|Solutions|Colligative properties
CORE IDEAS
Colligative effects depend on the effective number of dissolved particles rather than their chemical identity in the ideal dilute limit. Association and dissociation alter particle count.
FORMULAS
ΔTb = iKb m; ΔTf = iKf m; osmotic pressure π = iCRT. Here m is molality and C is molarity.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A non-electrolyte solution of molality 0.1 mol/kg in water with Kf = 1.86 K kg/mol has freezing-point depression 0.186 K.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not replace molality with molarity in freezing-point calculations. The van't Hoff factor need not be an integer in real solutions.
@@Chemistry|Surface Chemistry|Adsorption and colloids
CORE IDEAS
Adsorption concentrates a substance at an interface. Physisorption is dominated by intermolecular forces, while chemisorption involves chemical bonding. Colloids have a dispersed phase and a dispersion medium.
APPLICATION
Greater surface area often increases adsorption capacity. The Tyndall effect is scattering of light by a colloidal dispersion when optical contrast is sufficient.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Powdered activated carbon exposes far more surface than an equal mass in a compact block, supporting its use as an adsorbent.
COMMON MISTAKE
Adsorption is a surface phenomenon; absorption refers to uptake into the bulk. Both can occur together.
@@Mathematics|Quadratic Equation|Roots and coefficients
CORE IDEAS
A quadratic ax²+bx+c = 0 has roots related to its coefficients. The discriminant classifies real-root behaviour when the coefficients are real.
FORMULAS
α+β = −b/a; αβ = c/a; D = b²−4ac. Distinct real roots require D>0; a repeated real root requires D=0.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For x²−5x+6 = 0, roots are 2 and 3. Their squares sum to (α+β)²−2αβ = 25−12 = 13.
COMMON MISTAKE
Check that the coefficient of x² is nonzero before applying quadratic formulas.
@@Mathematics|Complex Number|Modulus argument and geometry
CORE IDEAS
A complex number z=x+iy corresponds to a point in the plane. Its modulus is distance from the origin; its argument is an angle with a stated branch convention.
FORMULAS
|z| = √(x²+y²); z z̄ = |z|²; |z−a| is the distance between z and a. Multiplication adds arguments modulo 2π.
WORKED EXAMPLE
The locus |z−(2+i)| = 3 is a circle centred at (2,1) with radius 3.
COMMON MISTAKE
Principal arguments do not always add directly without adjustment back into the chosen interval.
@@Mathematics|Functions|Domain range and mappings
CORE IDEAS
The domain lists permitted inputs; the codomain is specified in the definition; the range contains outputs actually attained. A function is injective if different inputs always give different outputs and surjective if its range equals its codomain.
METHOD
Check denominator restrictions, square-root conditions and logarithm arguments before manipulating expressions.
WORKED EXAMPLE
f(x)=x² from real numbers to nonnegative real numbers is onto but not one-to-one. Restricting the domain to nonnegative real numbers makes it bijective.
COMMON MISTAKE
A function's injectivity and surjectivity depend on its domain and codomain, not just its formula.
@@Mathematics|Limits|Evaluating limits
CORE IDEAS
A limit describes behaviour near a point and may exist even when the function is undefined at that point. Algebraic simplification, standard limits and squeezing are useful methods.
STANDARD LIMITS
lim(x→0) sinx/x = 1 with x in radians; lim(x→0) (exp(x)−1)/x = 1.
WORKED EXAMPLE
lim(x→2) (x²−4)/(x−2) = lim(x→2)(x+2) = 4. Cancelling the factor is valid for nearby x≠2.
COMMON MISTAKE
The form 0/0 is indeterminate, not a numerical answer. Angle units matter in trigonometric limits.
@@Mathematics|Application of Derivatives|Monotonicity and extrema
CORE IDEAS
The derivative gives instantaneous slope. A positive derivative throughout an interval implies increasing behaviour there. Candidate extrema occur at stationary points or points where differentiability fails; endpoints also matter on closed intervals.
METHOD
Find critical points, split the domain into intervals, inspect derivative signs and evaluate relevant endpoints.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For f(x)=x²−4x+1, f′(x)=2x−4. The derivative changes from negative to positive at x=2, giving a minimum f(2)=−3.
COMMON MISTAKE
A zero derivative alone does not prove a maximum or minimum; x³ has a stationary inflection at zero.
@@Mathematics|Definite Integration|Integration and signed area
CORE IDEAS
An antiderivative reverses differentiation. A definite integral accumulates signed contributions. Geometric area requires accounting for portions below the axis.
FORMULAS
∫xⁿdx = x^(n+1)/(n+1)+C for n≠−1; ∫1/x dx = ln|x|+C. If F′=f, then ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx = F(b)−F(a).
WORKED EXAMPLE
∫₀²(x−1)dx = 0, but the geometric area between y=x−1 and the x-axis from 0 to 2 is 1.
COMMON MISTAKE
A zero integral need not mean zero area: positive and negative contributions can cancel.
@@Mathematics|Differential Equations|Separable equations
CORE IDEAS
A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives. An initial condition selects a particular solution from a family. In a separable equation, variables can be grouped on opposite sides.
METHOD
For dy/dx = g(x)h(y), integrate dy/h(y) = g(x)dx where division is valid; separately check any solutions excluded by dividing by h(y).
WORKED EXAMPLE
dy/dx = 2xy gives ln|y| = x²+C for y≠0, so y=Aexp(x²). The solution y=0 is also included when A=0.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not lose constant solutions when dividing by an expression involving y.
@@Mathematics|Matrices|Matrix operations and inverses
CORE IDEAS
Matrix multiplication requires compatible dimensions and is generally noncommutative. A square matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant is nonzero.
FORMULA
For A=[[a,b],[c,d]], det A = ad−bc and A⁻¹ = [[d,−b],[−c,a]]/(ad−bc), when ad−bc≠0.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For A=[[1,2],[3,4]], determinant is −2 and inverse is [[−2,1],[1.5,−0.5]]. Multiplication by A gives the identity.
COMMON MISTAKE
AB and BA can differ or one may be undefined. Cancellation of a matrix factor requires appropriate invertibility.
@@Mathematics|Probability|Conditional probability and Bayes theorem
CORE IDEAS
Conditional probability updates an event probability after learning another event occurred. Independence means P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B), not that events cannot happen together.
FORMULAS
P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B) for P(B)>0. Bayes: P(Ai|B)=P(B|Ai)P(Ai)/ΣjP(B|Aj)P(Aj).
WORKED EXAMPLE
Two equally likely boxes contain red-ball fractions 1/4 and 3/4. Given a red draw, the probability the second box was chosen is (3/4)/(1/4+3/4)=3/4.
COMMON MISTAKE
P(A|B) and P(B|A) are generally different.
@@Mathematics|Permutation Combination|Counting selections and arrangements
CORE IDEAS
A permutation records order; a combination records selection without order. Use a product when independent stages are all required and a sum for mutually exclusive alternatives.
FORMULAS
nPr=n!/(n−r)!; nCr=n!/[r!(n−r)!].
WORKED EXAMPLE
Choose three students from five and appoint one of those three as captain. There are 5C3×3 = 30 outcomes.
COMMON MISTAKE
Check whether positions, roles or repetition make apparently identical selections distinct.
@@Mathematics|Three Dimensional Geometry|Lines planes and distances
CORE IDEAS
A line is described by a point and direction vector. A plane is described by a point and a normal vector. Parallelism and perpendicularity can be checked using vector products and dot products.
FORMULAS
Plane: ax+by+cz+d=0 with normal (a,b,c). Distance of (x₀,y₀,z₀) from plane is |ax₀+by₀+cz₀+d|/√(a²+b²+c²).
WORKED EXAMPLE
The distance of (1,2,3) from x+2y+2z−2=0 is |1+4+6−2|/3=3.
COMMON MISTAKE
A line's direction vector is not the same as a plane's normal unless the line is perpendicular to the plane.
@@Mathematics|Sequences and Series|Arithmetic and geometric progressions
CORE IDEAS
An arithmetic progression has a fixed difference; a geometric progression has a fixed ratio. Before summing a sequence, identify its pattern and indexing.
FORMULAS
AP: an=a+(n−1)d and Sn=n[2a+(n−1)d]/2. GP: Sn=a(1−rⁿ)/(1−r) for r≠1; infinite sum a/(1−r) requires |r|<1.
WORKED EXAMPLE
The first ten terms of the AP 3,7,11,... sum to 10[6+9×4]/2=210.
COMMON MISTAKE
The infinite geometric-sum formula is invalid when |r|≥1.
@@Biology|The Living World|Taxonomy and scientific names
CORE IDEAS
Taxonomy includes identification, nomenclature and classification. A species is placed within nested categories such as genus, family, order, class, phylum or division, and kingdom.
KEY DISTINCTION
In a binomial name, the genus begins with a capital letter and the specific epithet with a lowercase letter. Scientific names are italicised in print; the two words are separately underlined when handwritten.
WORKED EXAMPLE
In Homo sapiens, Homo is the genus and sapiens is the specific epithet. Together they identify the species name.
COMMON MISTAKE
A specific epithet alone is not the full scientific name and may occur in different genera.
@@Biology|Biological Classification|Viruses viroids and cellular organisms
CORE IDEAS
Bacteria are cellular prokaryotes. Viruses are acellular infectious entities that reproduce using host-cell machinery. Viroids are small infectious RNA agents without a protein coat.
COMPARE
A bacterium has cellular organisation, ribosomes and metabolism. A virus contains genetic material inside a protein capsid, with an envelope in some groups. Viroid structure is much simpler.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A question describing infectious naked RNA points toward a viroid rather than a conventional virus with a capsid.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not treat viruses as autonomous cells or assume all viruses contain both DNA and RNA as their genetic material.
@@Biology|Cell - The Unit of Life|Organelles and membrane transport
CORE IDEAS
The plasma membrane controls exchange between a cell and its surroundings. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
COMPARE
Mitochondria participate in aerobic energy metabolism; ribosomes synthesise proteins; the Golgi apparatus modifies and sorts cellular products. Chloroplasts perform photosynthesis in photosynthetic eukaryotes.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A cell that secretes large amounts of protein typically has substantial rough endoplasmic reticulum and an active Golgi apparatus.
COMMON MISTAKE
Ribosomes are not membrane-bound, and they occur in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
@@Biology|Cell Cycle and Cell Division|Mitosis and meiosis
CORE IDEAS
DNA is replicated in S phase before division. Mitosis generally preserves chromosome number; meiosis has one DNA replication followed by two divisions and reduces chromosome number.
KEY EVENTS
Homologous chromosomes pair during prophase I of meiosis. Homologues separate in anaphase I, while sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A diploid cell with 2n=8 produces haploid meiotic products with n=4 chromosomes. Immediately after S phase the chromosome count remains 8, although the DNA amount has doubled.
COMMON MISTAKE
Count chromosomes by centromeres, and distinguish chromosome number from DNA content.
@@Biology|Principles of Inheritance and Variation|Mendelian crosses
CORE IDEAS
Alleles segregate during gamete formation. Independent assortment applies to unlinked loci under the standard Mendelian assumptions. Dominance describes phenotype in a heterozygote, not the frequency or usefulness of an allele.
CROSS
For Aa×Aa with complete dominance, expected genotype ratio is 1 AA:2 Aa:1 aa and phenotype ratio is 3 dominant:1 recessive.
WORKED EXAMPLE
In a test cross Aa×aa, half the offspring are expected to be Aa and half aa, giving a 1:1 phenotypic ratio.
COMMON MISTAKE
Linkage, incomplete dominance and other mechanisms can change the familiar ratios.
@@Biology|Molecular Basis of Inheritance|Replication transcription and translation
CORE IDEAS
DNA replication copies genetic information. Transcription makes RNA using a DNA template; translation uses an mRNA sequence to specify an amino-acid sequence.
KEY DIRECTIONS
DNA and RNA polymerases extend a new strand in the 5′→3′ direction. The genetic code is read as successive mRNA codons during translation.
WORKED EXAMPLE
For a DNA template segment 3′-TAC-5′, the corresponding RNA segment is 5′-AUG-3′, which can serve as a start codon in an appropriate context.
COMMON MISTAKE
A template strand and a coding strand have different relationships to the RNA sequence. State which strand is given.
@@Biology|Photosynthesis in Higher Plants|Light reactions and carbon fixation
CORE IDEAS
Light reactions convert light energy into chemical forms and release oxygen from water. Carbon fixation incorporates carbon dioxide into organic molecules through linked biochemical reactions.
KEY DISTINCTION
ATP and reducing power produced by light reactions support carbon assimilation. C3 and C4 plants differ in the first stable products and spatial organisation of initial carbon fixation.
WORKED EXAMPLE
In a tracing experiment, oxygen released during photosynthesis originates from water rather than directly from carbon dioxide.
COMMON MISTAKE
Calling carbon fixation a dark reaction does not mean it occurs only at night; it depends on products and regulation associated with light reactions.
@@Biology|Human Health and Diseases|Immunity and infectious disease
CORE IDEAS
Innate immunity provides broad early defences. Adaptive immunity includes antigen-specific responses and memory. B cells can give rise to antibody-secreting plasma cells; T cells have several regulatory and effector roles.
COMPARE
A vaccine prepares an adaptive response without requiring the full natural disease course. An antibiotic acts against susceptible bacteria and does not directly cure a viral infection.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A faster, stronger response on re-exposure to an antigen illustrates immunological memory.
COMMON MISTAKE
An infectious disease's pathogen, vector and transmission route are distinct concepts. For malaria, distinguish Plasmodium from its mosquito vector.
@@Biology|Human Reproduction|Gametogenesis and fertilisation
CORE IDEAS
Meiosis produces haploid gametes. Fertilisation restores diploidy when haploid gamete nuclei combine. Development proceeds through cleavage and later differentiation.
KEY DISTINCTION
Spermatogenesis yields multiple functional sperm from a primary spermatocyte, while oogenesis partitions most cytoplasm into one large functional product and polar bodies.
WORKED EXAMPLE
In humans, normal gametes contain 23 chromosomes; a typical zygote formed by their union contains 46.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not confuse ovulation, fertilisation and implantation: they are different events at different sites and times.
@@Biology|Body Fluids and Circulation|The cardiac cycle
CORE IDEAS
The heart generates pressure differences that move blood. Valves limit reverse flow. Pulmonary circulation links the heart and lungs; systemic circulation links the heart and other tissues.
KEY RELATION
Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A heart rate of 70 beats/min and stroke volume 70 mL/beat give an output of 4900 mL/min, or 4.9 L/min.
COMMON MISTAKE
Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry blood toward it. Oxygen content does not define whether a vessel is an artery or vein.
@@Biology|Biotechnology - Principles and Processes|Recombinant DNA and PCR
CORE IDEAS
Recombinant DNA methods combine and propagate selected DNA sequences. A vector carries an insert into a suitable host. Restriction enzymes recognise specific sequences; DNA ligase joins compatible DNA ends.
PCR CYCLE
Denaturation separates DNA strands, annealing allows primers to bind, and extension uses a DNA polymerase to copy the target.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Under ideal doubling, one target molecule becomes 2¹⁰=1024 copies after ten PCR cycles. Real reactions can be less efficient.
COMMON MISTAKE
PCR amplifies DNA without requiring whole-cell reproduction. Primer design and target specificity still matter.
@@Biology|Ecosystem|Energy flow and trophic levels
CORE IDEAS
Producers capture energy; consumers obtain it through feeding. Energy flows through ecosystems and is dissipated as heat, while nutrients cycle between organisms and their surroundings.
KEY DISTINCTION
Net primary productivity is gross primary productivity minus producer respiration. An ecological energy pyramid is upright because usable energy decreases across trophic transfers.
WORKED EXAMPLE
If producers make 1000 units of gross production and respire 400, net primary production is 600 units available for growth and other transfers.
COMMON MISTAKE
A rule-of-thumb transfer efficiency is not a universal fixed value for every organism or ecosystem.
@@Biology|Biodiversity and its Conservation|Conservation approaches
CORE IDEAS
Biodiversity includes variation within species, between species and across ecosystems. Habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species and other pressures can reduce biodiversity.
COMPARE
In situ conservation protects populations within their habitats. Ex situ conservation protects selected organisms or genetic resources outside their natural habitats, such as seed banks.
WORKED EXAMPLE
A protected forest reserve is an in situ approach; a seed bank preserving crop varieties is an ex situ approach.
COMMON MISTAKE
A high number of individuals of one species does not by itself imply high species diversity.
